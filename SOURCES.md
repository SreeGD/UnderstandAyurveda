# Sources

What this app's content is built on, and — just as importantly — what was deliberately left out.

Classical citations (Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridayam) are given at **chapter
level**, not verse level. A precise verse number that turns out to be wrong is worse than an honest
chapter reference: it manufactures authority the reader cannot easily check. This is the failure
mode gate C4 exists to expose.

## Modern sources used

| Source | What it is | What it is cited for |
|---|---|---|
| **NCCIH, US National Institutes of Health** — *Ayurvedic Medicine: An Introduction* | US government health body | Safety findings, evidence quality, how practitioners actually assess, prakriti stability, dosha–element pairings |
| **Vasant Lad** (Ayurvedic Institute) — *Ayurveda: A Brief Introduction and Guide* | Named modern authority | Constitution determined at conception and stable across life; the three doshas as functional energies |
| **European Institute of Vedic Studies** — *Pancha Karma* course manual | Practitioner training curriculum | That panchakarma is a staged clinical procedure, not a spa treatment |
| **Better Health Channel**, Victoria State Government | Reputable public health body | General framing only |

### What was taken from NCCIH specifically

The NIH fact sheet is the strongest source in the set and carries findings that materially
strengthen this app's safety posture:

- An NCCAM-funded study found that of 70 over-the-counter Ayurvedic remedies, **14 contained lead,
  mercury, and/or arsenic at levels that could be harmful**. The CDC linked 12 lead-poisoning cases
  over a three-year period to Ayurvedic medications. This is now stated directly on every herb
  entry — it is a far better argument for this app's no-dosing rule than editorial assertion.
- **Pregnant and nursing people should consult their provider** before Ayurvedic therapy — cited on
  the pregnancy red-flag path.
- **Diagnosis should come from a provider with substantial conventional medical training** — cited
  where the app declines to diagnose.
- Clinical trials of Ayurvedic approaches have been **small, with design problems and inadequate
  controls**; evidence varies and more rigorous research is needed. This is now stated plainly in
  the opening lesson rather than left implicit.

Note that NCCIH also links each dosha to susceptibility to named diseases (rheumatoid arthritis,
hypertension, diabetes, cancer, asthma). **None of that was imported.** Gate C8 blocks disease
claims, and correctly — an educational app has no business telling someone their constitution
predisposes them to cancer.

## What was deliberately excluded

Of 25 documents in the source set, 21 were not used as citations.

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| *Ayurveda.pdf* | A clinic patient testimonial — weight loss, "detox", citing Deepak Chopra. Marketing, not evidence. |
| 9 × *Newsletter* issues | One devotional organisation's newsletter run (ISKCON Health and Welfare Ministry). Not an authority on Ayurvedic doctrine. |
| *Bhaktivedanta Hospital*, *Maharishi Ayurveda* | Institutional promotional material. |
| *Be Healthy, Happy and Holy — Sleep by 10 PM* | Devotional. The 10pm claim is sourced instead to the classical daily-routine chapters, which actually argue for it. |
| *Complete Handbook of Nature Cure*, *The Complete Raw Juice Therapy*, *How To Sleep Less* | Naturopathy and self-help — a different tradition, and unreliable. |
| *Manual Ayurveda 2006* | A Spanish massage-college course manual. |
| *Indian Medicinal Plants* (836pp), *Ayurveda Herbs*, *Acalypha indica* | Herb and materia medica references. This app carries **no dosing or preparation guidance anywhere**, and gate C8 admits no exception on herb entries. Botanical names were the only thing worth taking, and those are already standard. |
| *Ayurveda, Yoga*; several newsletters | Scanned images with no extractable text. |

## The "5,000 years" claim

Three documents in the set date Ayurveda to "5,000 years ago" or more. This is repeated widely and
is **not** the scholarly consensus — standard dating places the compilation of the Charaka Samhita
somewhere between roughly 400 BCE and 200 CE, with older oral antecedents. The app says "roughly two
thousand years" and marks the dating question as contested rather than picking the more impressive
number.

This is a small thing, and it is exactly the kind of small thing that indicates whether a source is
being careful.

## Standing limitation

None of this makes the content practitioner-certified. It is sourced and attributed; it has not been
reviewed by a credentialed Ayurvedic practitioner. That remains a pre-launch gate, and the app says
so on its face.
